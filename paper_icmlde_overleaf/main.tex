\title{Real-Time Adaptive Cross-Media Recommendation Using Semantic Embeddings, Knowledge Graph Scoring, and Graph Collaborative Learning}

\author[a]{Sanjay A. K.}

\address[a]{Department of Computer Science and Engineering, Amrita School of Computing, Coimbatore, Amrita Vishwa Vidyapeetham, India}

\begin{abstract}
A user's interests span media, yet most recommenders operate within single-domain silos. We built a real-time recommender that puts movies, TV shows, books, music, and health, industrial, and finance products into one catalog of 120,747 items. Candidates come from Sentence-BERT similarity (searched with Qdrant) and from creator and title matching; a hybrid score reranks them with an exponential moving average (EMA) of recent feedback, LightGCN collaborative filtering, and knowledge-graph (KG) and profile scores. On three Amazon Reviews 2023 categories with up to 50,000 users, the hybrid with validation-selected weights is the best of all 16 combinations of its four signals on two categories, improving NDCG@20 over the strongest single signal by 21.6\% and 26.9\%, and removing any signal lowers it significantly; on the third category, entity overlap alone performs best. A recommendation over the full catalog takes 363~ms at the median on a laptop, and a feedback event updates the user profile in 0.05~ms.
\end{abstract}

\section{Introduction}
\label{sec:introduction}
\email{g\_krishnapriya@cb.amrita.edu (Krishna Priya G.), pbsk@cb.amrita.edu (Bagavathi Sivakumar P.)}
Recommender systems are the main interface through which users discover digital content, yet most deployed systems remain domain-specific: a movie platform recommends films, a music platform recommends tracks, and a book platform recommends books. Human interest does not follow these boundaries. A user interested in science fiction may enjoy a film such as \emph{Interstellar}, a novel about space exploration, a soundtrack with a similar tone, and educational content on astronomy, so a useful recommender should connect these signals across media.

This paper presents a working cross-media recommendation pipeline for movies, TV shows, books, and music, which has since been extended to health, industrial, and finance products. Every item is mapped to one schema and one semantic retrieval layer: Sentence-BERT compares items of different domains through their descriptions \cite{reimers2019sbert}, Qdrant provides nearest-neighbor retrieval \cite{qdrant}, LightGCN learns collaborative preference \cite{he2020lightgcn}, an exponential moving average (EMA) of item embeddings adapts to each feedback event, and an in-memory knowledge graph (KG) adds entity- and profile-aware scoring, behind a FastAPI backend \cite{fastapi} with MySQL logging.

These capabilities are rarely combined in one multi-domain pipeline: graph models are usually benchmarked on single-domain datasets, semantic systems retrieve by meaning rather than by preference, cross-domain methods typically transfer between two domains instead of normalizing many into one catalog, and real-time personalization is usually separate from graph learning. This paper makes three contributions:
\begin{enumerate}
    \item An integrated cross-media platform with a unified catalog of seven content types that supports semantic, creator-aware, and title search.
    \item A hybrid ranking model that combines semantic retrieval, EMA adaptation, LightGCN graph scoring, and KG/profile scoring, with explainable sub-scores.
    \item A quantitative evaluation on three public interaction datasets, comprising single-signal baselines, an ablation over all 16 combinations of the four signals, validation-based weight selection, latency measurements, and scenario tests of the deployed system.
\end{enumerate}

\section{Related Work and Novelty}
\label{sec:discussion}
Collaborative filtering learns from behavior and content-based recommendation from item metadata, and hybrid approaches combine the two to reduce the cold-start problem \cite{zhang2019deep}. NGCF applies graph convolution to the user--item graph, and LightGCN simplifies it by keeping only neighborhood propagation \cite{wang2019ngcf,he2020lightgcn}; here LightGCN is a reranking signal for users with interaction history rather than the candidate generator. Sequential recommenders such as SASRec model the order of user actions within a single domain \hlon \cite{kang2018sasrec}\hloff{}, and Sentence-BERT embeddings \cite{reimers2019sbert} let items of different media share one vector space before any interactions exist. KGAT and RippleNet propagate preferences over knowledge-graph relations such as directed-by \cite{wang2019kgat,wang2018ripplenet}. Cross-domain recommendation transfers preferences between domains, mostly between two \cite{zang2022cross}, and a recent survey extends the scope to multi-domain transfer \cite{zhang2025survey}; large language models augmented with graph structure have also been used for relevance and explanation \cite{wu2024llmrec}. Studies from Amrita Vishwa Vidyapeetham have addressed cold-start prediction, collaborative filtering, sparse data, dual-feedback personalization, and community-aware recommendation \cite{amrita2023densenet,amrita2023adamvrgo,amrita2024sparse,amrita2025dualfeedback,amrita2026svd}, but not the integration of heterogeneous media into one pipeline.

Recent work has moved in two directions that are directly relevant here. Knowledge-aware graph recommenders combine LightGCN-style propagation with knowledge graphs: LGKAT adds a personalized knowledge-aware attention sub-network to LightGCN and is evaluated separately on movie, book, music, and restaurant data \hlon \cite{hassanzadeh2025lgkat}\hloff{}, and LightKG simplifies the graph neural network of knowledge-aware recommenders to reduce training cost on sparse data \hlon \cite{li2025lightkg}\hloff{}. Cross-domain recommenders increasingly use large language models as a semantic bridge between domains: LLMCDSR generates pseudo-interactions with an LLM and injects collaborative information into textual item features \hlon \cite{xin2025llmcdsr}\hloff{}, LLM4CDSR builds unified LLM-based item representations and hierarchical user profiles across domains \hlon \cite{liu2025llm4cdsr}\hloff{}, LeCDSR likewise enhances cross-domain sequential recommendation with an LLM \hlon \cite{wang2026lecdsr}\hloff{}, and KLLMRec augments multi-domain recommendation with LLM-derived knowledge \hlon \cite{li2026kllmrec}\hloff{}.

\section{Implementation}
\label{sec:implementation}
\subsection{System overview}
\label{sec:overview}
In the architecture of Fig.~\ref{fig:architecture}, each source is mapped into the unified catalog, embedded, and loaded into Qdrant and MySQL offline, and LightGCN is retrained periodically. At request time, the FastAPI backend validates a query together with an optional user, content-type or domain filter, viewer age, and result count $K$; it matches creators and titles, retrieves Qdrant candidates, reranks them, and returns the final score with every sub-score, which the React frontend displays. Feedback such as likes, skips, and bookmarks is logged through the interaction endpoint and updates the user's EMA profile immediately. Before ranking, the declared age and domain remove ineligible items: a viewer who gives no age is treated as a teenager, and health, industrial, and finance items count as adult content by default.

\subsection{Unified catalog and data sources}
\label{sec:catalog}
Each domain $d\in\mathcal{D}$ provides a mapper $\phi_d$ from a raw row $x$ to the shared item representation $r_i$, and the catalog is
\begin{equation}
    \mathcal{C}=\bigcup_{d\in\mathcal{D}}\{\phi_d(x):x\in X_d\},
    \quad
    r_i=(id,type,title,desc,creators,categories,text_i).
    \label{eq:catalog_union}
\end{equation}
To add a domain we only write a new $\phi_d$; embedding, search, KG scoring, the API, and the frontend stay the same. Movies and TV shows use TMDb metadata and credits \cite{tmdb}; books use the Open Library APIs \cite{openlibrary}; music combines a public Spotify songs export with current tracks from the Last.fm API \cite{lastfm}; and health, industrial, and finance products come from the Amazon Reviews 2023 metadata \cite{hou2024blair}, with finance also including accounting and personal-finance titles from Open Library. The deployed catalog holds 120,747 items: 31,133 songs, 23,492 movies, 20,000 health and 20,000 industrial products, 12,402 books, 10,617 TV shows, and 3,103 finance items. Directors and cast, authors, and artists all map to the shared creators field, which therefore serves both person search and the knowledge graph.

\subsection{Semantic retrieval and creator and title matching}
\label{sec:person}
Each item's embedding text (title, categories, description, and creators) and each query are encoded by the all-MiniLM-L6-v2 Sentence-BERT model into 384-dimensional vectors \cite{reimers2019sbert}. The users' EMA profiles (Section~\ref{sec:ema}) live in the same space, so users, items, and queries are compared directly. With $\mathbf{e}_i$ the embedding of item $i$ and $\mathbf{q}$ that of the query, the semantic score is the cosine similarity $S_{\mathit{sem}}(i,q)=\mathbf{e}_i^\top\mathbf{q}/(\|\mathbf{e}_i\|\,\|\mathbf{q}\|)$. Qdrant performs approximate nearest-neighbor search over this similarity with an HNSW index; it generates candidates rather than the final ranking and lets a new item be recommended from its metadata alone.

Dense similarity does not capture the intent of a name or a short title such as \emph{F1}, so two deterministic stages run first. Creator matching looks for exact, prefix, and substring matches on normalized creator names and places up to five matching items first. Title matching assigns each item one of five tiers on whole normalized words: the query equals the title (tier 0), the title starts with the query (1) or contains it (2), every query token occurs in the title or categories (3), or the query phrase occurs in the description (4). Tiers 0 and 1 rank first and tiers 2--4 are ordered by popularity; lexical matches fill at most $\max(3,\lfloor K/2\rfloor)$ slots and receive the fixed score $1.30-0.06\,t$ for tier $t$, which keeps them above the cosine band. Semantic candidates fill the remaining slots after deduplication.

\subsection{Interaction logging and EMA adaptation}
\label{sec:ema}
\hlon Interactions are logged in MySQL with a signed weight $w$: view $0.4$, click $0.6$, like and bookmark $1.0$, complete $1.2$, and skip $-1.0$; an explicit rating $r\in[1,5]$ maps to $w=(r-3)/2$, and events with $w=0$ are ignored.\hloff{} Let $\mathbf{u}_t\in\mathbb{R}^{384}$ be the user's profile and $\alpha=0.25$ the smoothing factor. \hlon An event with weight $w$ on item $i$ moves the profile by the step $\eta=\alpha\min(|w|,1)$ toward $\mathrm{sign}(w)\,\mathbf{e}_i$:\hloff{}
\begin{equation}
    \tilde{\mathbf{u}}_{t+1}=(1-\eta)\,\mathbf{u}_t+\eta\,\mathrm{sign}(w)\,\mathbf{e}_i,
    \qquad
    \mathbf{u}_{t+1}=\tilde{\mathbf{u}}_{t+1}/\|\tilde{\mathbf{u}}_{t+1}\|.
    \label{eq:ema_update}
\end{equation}
\hlon The profile is empty until the user's first non-neutral event on an embedded item, which sets $\mathbf{u}_1=\mathrm{sign}(w)\,\mathbf{e}_i$, so users without events receive no EMA score. A skip or a low rating moves the profile away from the item,\hloff{} a view moves it only slightly, and the profile is rescaled to unit length after each step. The EMA score is the cosine similarity $S_{\mathit{ema}}(i,u)=\mathbf{e}_i^\top\mathbf{u}_t/(\|\mathbf{e}_i\|\,\|\mathbf{u}_t\|)$. An event changes only this one vector, so the profile follows a change of taste within a session without retraining.

\subsection{Graph collaborative filtering with LightGCN}
\label{sec:lightgcn}
We train LightGCN \cite{he2020lightgcn} on the interaction log. \hlon Every event with $w>0$ adds an unweighted edge to the bipartite graph $\mathcal{G}=(\mathcal{U}\cup\mathcal{I},\mathcal{E})$; repeated events on one pair give a single edge, and skips and low ratings are left out instead of serving as negatives.\hloff{} With $\mathcal{N}_u$ the neighbors of user $u$, layer $\ell$ propagates and the final embedding averages all $L$ layers,
\begin{equation}
    \mathbf{e}^{(\ell)}_u=\sum_{i\in\mathcal{N}_u}\frac{\mathbf{e}^{(\ell-1)}_i}{\sqrt{|\mathcal{N}_u|\,|\mathcal{N}_i|}},
    \qquad
    \mathbf{e}_u=\frac{1}{L+1}\sum_{\ell=0}^{L}\mathbf{e}^{(\ell)}_u,
    \label{eq:lightgcn}
\end{equation}
and in the same way for items. We train it with the Bayesian Personalized Ranking (BPR) loss
\begin{equation}
    \mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{BPR}}=
    -\sum_{(u,i,j)\in\mathcal{D}}\ln\sigma(\hat{y}_{ui}-\hat{y}_{uj})
    +\lambda\|\Theta\|^2,
    \label{eq:bpr}
\end{equation}
where $\hat{y}_{ui}=\mathbf{e}_u^\top\mathbf{e}_i$ and $\Theta$ holds the layer-0 embeddings of the batch. \hlon For each positive pair $(u,i)$ one negative $j$ is drawn uniformly from the catalog, and drawn again if $(u,j)$ is observed.\hloff{} At ranking time the graph score is the cosine of the $\ell_2$-normalized embeddings, $S_{\mathit{graph}}(i,u)=\mathbf{e}_u^\top\mathbf{e}_i/(\|\mathbf{e}_u\|\,\|\mathbf{e}_i\|)$. A user or item missing from the training graph gets no graph score, and the other signals rank its candidates. Section~\ref{sec:setup} gives the training settings.

\subsection{Knowledge graph and profile-aware scoring}
\label{sec:kg}
\hlon The knowledge graph is built in memory from the catalog, without a separate graph database, in three steps (Fig.~\ref{fig:knowledge_graph}).\hloff{}
\hlon \emph{(i) Entities.}\hloff{} Each item becomes a node typed by its content type. After Unicode normalization and case-folding, each creator becomes a Creator node, each category a Genre/topic node, and each alphanumeric token of at least three characters in the title, description, creators, categories, and content type a term entity of the item. Each user becomes a User node, and the declared profile attributes become Profession, Age group, and Interest/skill nodes.
\hlon \emph{(ii) Relations.}\hloff{} Items link to creators by directed\_by, written\_by, or performed\_by, according to the content type, and to genres by belongs\_to\_genre; users link to items by liked, bookmarked, or completed when the event weight is positive (these edges also form the LightGCN graph) and to their profile nodes. Each profession and age group expands into a fixed list of related topic terms (related\_to\_profession and suitable\_for; e.g., \emph{data analyst} $\rightarrow$ data, analytics, business, statistics, technology, science), and an age group may also carry unsuitable terms (e.g., \emph{family} $\rightarrow$ horror, crime, thriller, war).
\hlon \emph{(iii) Paths.}\hloff{} A user reaches an item through a length-two path via a shared entity, and because entity namespaces are shared by all domains, the path can end at an item of any medium:
\begin{equation}
    P(u,i)=\{u\rightarrow e\rightarrow i:\;
    e\in \mathcal{E}_{\mathit{query}}\cup\mathcal{E}_{\mathit{creator}}\cup\mathcal{E}_{\mathit{genre}}\cup\mathcal{E}_{\mathit{profile}}\}.
    \label{eq:paths}
\end{equation}
\hlon The two scores below count such paths through set intersections, without enumerating them.\hloff{}

The KG score counts paths through query-term entities. With $\mathcal{T}(q)$ and $\mathcal{T}(i)$ the term sets of query $q$ and item $i$,
\begin{equation}
    S_{\mathit{kg}}(i,q)=
    \min\!\left(1,\frac{|\mathcal{T}(q)\cap\mathcal{T}(i)|}
    {\max(3,|\mathcal{T}(q)|)}\right),
    \label{eq:kg_score}
\end{equation}
where the floor of three prevents a one- or two-word query from reaching the maximum through a single shared term. The profile score rewards items that match the user's declared attributes and penalizes unsuitable ones:
\begin{equation}
    S_{\mathit{profile}}(i,u)=
    \sum_{t\in\mathcal{P}_{+}(u)} \delta(t,i)w_{+}
    -\sum_{t\in\mathcal{P}_{-}(u)} \delta(t,i)w_{-}
    +\mathbf{1}[\mathrm{pref}(u)=\mathrm{type}(i)]w_c,
    \label{eq:profile_score}
\end{equation}
where $\mathcal{P}_{+}(u)$ holds the user's interests, skills, and the terms expanded from the profession and age group, $\mathcal{P}_{-}(u)$ the unsuitable terms, $\delta(t,i)=1$ if term $t$ occurs in the text of item $i$, $\mathrm{pref}(u)$ the preferred content types, $w_+=0.16$, $w_-=0.25$, and $w_c=0.15$; the score is clipped to $[-1,1]$. The profile uses only declared, consent-based attributes, not private social-media data.

\subsection{Hybrid ranking}
\label{sec:hybrid}
Candidates are sorted by the hybrid score
\begin{equation}
    S(i,u,q)=S_{\mathit{sem}}(i,q)+B_{\mathit{pref}}(i,u)+
    \lambda_gS_{\mathit{graph}}(i,u)+
    \lambda_eS_{\mathit{ema}}(i,u)+
    \lambda_kS_{\mathit{kg}}(i,q)+
    \lambda_pS_{\mathit{profile}}(i,u),
    \label{eq:hybrid}
\end{equation}
where $S_{\mathit{sem}}$ is replaced by the fixed lexical score for creator and title matches. $B_{\mathit{pref}}$ is a short-term preference bonus from the user's 500 most recent events: with $c_u(g)$ and $c_u(\tau)$ the summed event weights of category $g$ and content type $\tau$,
\begin{equation}
    B_{\mathit{pref}}(i,u)=0.035\!\sum_{g\in\mathrm{cat}(i)}\!\min(c_u(g),3)+0.02\min(c_u(\mathrm{type}(i)),3)-0.15\,\mathbf{1}[i\in\mathcal{I}^{+}_u]-0.35\,\mathbf{1}[i\in\mathcal{I}^{-}_u],
    \label{eq:pref}
\end{equation}
with $\mathcal{I}^{+}_u$ and $\mathcal{I}^{-}_u$ holding the items this user has already rated positively or negatively; both are demoted so that unseen items rise. For known users, $5K$ candidates are retrieved before reranking, so that personalization can promote items from outside the initial top $K$. The deployed system uses the hand-set weights $\lambda_g=0.20$, $\lambda_e=0.15$, $\lambda_k=0.08$, and $\lambda_p=0.10$; Section~\ref{sec:results} compares them with weights selected on a validation split.

\section{Experimental Setup}
\label{sec:setup}
An offline benchmark on public interaction data measures every signal of Eq.~\eqref{eq:hybrid} under identical conditions; scenario tests of the deployed system cover the query- and profile-dependent behavior.

\emph{Datasets.} \hlon The benchmark uses three categories of the Amazon Reviews 2023 corpus \cite{hou2024blair}: Movies \& TV, Health \& Household, and Industrial \& Scientific.\hloff{} Ratings of four or five stars are positive interactions; other ratings are discarded. The two large categories are filtered to a 10-core graph and a random sample of 50,000 users is drawn, after which items with fewer than five remaining interactions are removed; Industrial \& Scientific does not survive a 10-core filter and uses the 5-core graph without sampling. Item text (title, category path, description, and store) comes from the corpus metadata. Table~\ref{tab:datasets} lists the graphs, whose densities are about 0.04\%; metadata covers every Health \& Household and Industrial \& Scientific item but only 67.3\% of the Movies \& TV items, and items without text receive no semantic, EMA, or KG signal.

\emph{Split, candidates, and metrics.} \hlon We sort each user's positive interactions by time: the last two go to the test set, the one before them to the validation set, and LightGCN is trained on the rest only.\hloff{} Each method scores every catalog item the user has not interacted with, so no negatives are sampled during evaluation. \hlon Recall@$K$, NDCG@$K$, MAP@$K$, and MRR@$K$ are averaged over test users for $K\in\{10,20\}$\hloff{} (HitRate@$K$ and Precision@$K$ are computed as well), and differences in per-user NDCG@20 are tested with the Wilcoxon signed-rank test, paired by user.

\emph{Offline signals.} Public logs contain neither typed queries nor declared profiles, so each signal uses its closest offline counterpart: $S_{\mathit{sem}}$ takes the user's most recent history item as the query, which corresponds to the system's similar-item endpoint; $S_{\mathit{ema}}$ replays Eq.~\eqref{eq:ema_update} over the history in time order; $S_{\mathit{graph}}$ is the LightGCN cosine score; and $S_{\mathit{kg}}$ applies Eq.~\eqref{eq:kg_score} to the entities of the user's history (category-path nodes, store, and main category), normalized by the candidate's entity count because a history holds far more entities than a query. Entities occurring on a single item or on more than half of the items are dropped. The declared-profile score has no offline counterpart and is covered by the scenarios, and $B_{\mathit{pref}}$ is omitted because its category term overlaps with the offline KG signal.

\emph{Baselines, combinations, and weight selection.} \hlon The popularity baseline ignores both content and the user, and each single-signal baseline uses one kind of evidence:\hloff{} the semantic one matches the latest item (content only, so it needs no interactions), EMA the session profile (recent taste), LightGCN co-interactions only, and KG shared categories and brands. \hlon The ablation evaluates all $2^4=16$ combinations of the four signals, from the empty one (popularity alone) to all four, plus the hybrid with the hand-set weights of Section~\ref{sec:hybrid}.\hloff{} \hlon Each combination has its own weights, chosen by grid search on 10,000 validation users\hloff{} to maximize NDCG@20 (the first signal has weight 1 and every other signal a weight in $\{0.05,0.1,0.2,0.4,0.8,1.6,3.2\}$), so that no combination is handicapped by weights chosen for another; the test set is used once.

\emph{Training settings.} \hlon Table~\ref{tab:settings} lists the settings. LightGCN is trained for a fixed 60 epochs of $\lceil|\mathcal{E}|/B\rceil$ mini-batches without early stopping, so that neither validation nor test data influence training.\hloff{} All runs use seed 42 on one NVIDIA T4 GPU, on which a training run takes 17.0, 13.2, and 1.2 minutes on the three graphs.

\hlon On Movies \& TV and Industrial \& Scientific, the four-signal hybrid is the best of the 16 combinations on all four measures at both cut-offs.\hloff{} It improves NDCG@20 over the strongest single signal, semantic similarity, by 21.6\% and 26.9\% (most-popular: factors of 2.3 and 1.9) and HitRate@20 from 0.051 to 0.063 and from 0.068 to 0.089. \hlon Over all 700 requests, the median latency is 363~ms (mean 371~ms, p95 455~ms, p99 507~ms).\hloff{} Personalized requests are 28~ms slower at the median than anonymous ones: the MySQL preference bonus (mean 6.4~ms), LightGCN (0.07~ms), and EMA steps together add about 15~ms, and fetching $5K$ instead of $K$ candidates accounts for the rest. Updating the EMA profile takes 0.05~ms, so the next request already reflects a like or a skip, with no retraining. Most of the time goes to the lexical title scan (191~ms), a pandas pass over the catalog on every request, and to the Qdrant query (83~ms), which returns up to $5K$ candidates with their payloads. Because the CPU-bound scan is serialized by the Python interpreter lock, \hlon one API process sustains 2.7 requests per second, and 3.3 with eight concurrent workers\hloff{}; an inverted title index and several worker processes are the main routes to higher throughput.

\section{Conclusion and Future Work}
\label{sec:conclusion}
We have described a real-time adaptive cross-media recommender that ranks seven content types from one catalog by combining semantic, creator and title, EMA, LightGCN, and KG and profile signals. On three Amazon Reviews 2023 categories, the hybrid with validation-selected weights was the best of all 16 signal combinations on two of them, the semantic and LightGCN signals contributed most, and weight selection improved on the hand-set weights; \hlon a request takes 363~ms at the median and a feedback update 0.05~ms\hloff{}. Three limitations remain: entity overlap alone was best on Health \& Household, the deployed LightGCN model covers only the demo users, and one process serves about three requests per second. We plan to tune the weights per domain, add an inverted title index, and extend the system to more domains and user studies.
